Part 1 · covers search as an analytics tool: BM25, cosine, k1 and b

BM25 and cosine: two ways to rank the same matches

5 min live

Finding the complaints that contain "late", "fee" or "refund" is easy: 274 of the 3,000 do. Deciding which of the 274 to read first is the hard part, and two standard rankings disagree about it. The difference comes down to one question: how much is the fifth mention of "fee" worth compared with the first?

LiveBM25 in words: three dials on one score2 min▶

BM25 scores a complaint against a query by adding up one contribution per query word the complaint contains. Each contribution is the product of three ideas you already know from session 2.

Rare words count more (idf). The bench uses Lucene's version, idf = ln(1 + (N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5)), where N is 3,000 complaints and df is how many contain the word. "Refund" outranks "account" because fewer complaints use it.

Repeats saturate (k1). The term-frequency part is tf(k1 + 1) / (tf + k1). The first mention is worth 1; every further mention adds less, and the total can never pass k1 + 1. The figure above draws exactly that curve from the formula.

Long texts are discounted (b). Inside the same fraction, k1 is multiplied by (1 - b + b times length / average length). With b = 1 a complaint twice the average length needs more mentions to score the same; with b = 0 length is ignored. The bench indexes the first 50 words of each complaint, after stop words and xxxx come out, so lengths vary less than they would in full text; on your own long documents b has more to do.

LiveThe defaults disagree, and that is the lesson1 min▶

Manning, Raghavan and Schutze give the textbook setting: "reasonable values are to set k1 and k3 to a value between 1.2 and 2 and b = 0.75" (Introduction to Information Retrieval, 2008). They also note that "a k1 value of 0 corresponds to a binary model".

Robertson and Zaragoza, writing about the framework BM25 came from, say "a common combination would be b = 0.5 and k1 = 2", and that "many experiments suggest a somewhat lower value of k1 and a somewhat higher value of b" (Foundations and Trends in IR 3(4): 333-389, 2009). So one source suggests a b of 0.75, another reports 0.5 as common and then reports experiments pulling the other way.

Nobody here is wrong. The best k1 and b depend on how long your documents are and how people write them, which is why the bench ships with 1.2 and 0.75 and lets you move both. The professional move is to write down the values you chose, and to change them only after checking a set of queries whose right answers you know.

LiveFour real queries, four lessons2 min▶

"late fee refund": same matches, two rankings. 274 complaints contain at least one of the three words. BM25 puts complaint 6594476 (vehicle loan) first; cosine on TF-IDF puts 7449382 (checking or savings, an annual fee on an IRA) first. Only three ids appear in both top fives. Set k1 to 0 and BM25's top five reshuffles again, to 7668748, 6594476, 6608852, 6645804, 7142738.

"repossessed my car": the easy case. 139 matches, and every complaint in both top fives is a vehicle loan complaint. When the vocabulary is specific, any reasonable ranking works.

"identity theft inquiry": the top five are one template. 284 matches. The top five under both rankings are five different complaints (6697815, 7166396, 7215809, 7252034, 7270412) whose opening words are identical, "i am a victim of identity theft please remove this fraud inquiry from my", all tied at a BM25 score of 11.825. Templated complaints are common in credit reporting, and search is often how you discover them. They are also the near-duplicate risk from session 4: copies of one letter can sit on both sides of a train and test split.

"zelle scam": a query can only find words the index kept. Only "scam" survives normalising (13 complaints). "Zelle" is not in the index at all, because it never appears in the first 50 words of any of the 3,000 complaints. The search did not fail; the session 1 decision to keep 50 words did.

Self-studyCosine on TF-IDF: the other ranking3 min▶

Jurafsky and Martin put it plainly: "By far the most common similarity metric is the cosine" (Speech and Language Processing, 2026 draft, ch 5). Cosine treats the query and each complaint as TF-IDF vectors and scores the angle between them. Because each complaint vector is divided by its own length, a short complaint dense in one query word scores high, and a word repeated five times keeps adding weight in proportion.

BM25 makes the opposite bets: repeats saturate, and length is discounted by a dial you control. Neither is the true ranking. For Ledgerly the useful habit is to run both on a handful of queries, read the disagreements, and pick the one whose top five your complaint handlers would have picked.

Self-studyWhere search goes next (and which side this session takes)2 min▶

Here search is an analytics tool: a person types a query to count, read and monitor complaints across a corpus. Two neighbouring jobs use the same formula for different ends. Working BM25 by hand to retrieve the right policy clause for an assistant lives in Ship an Internal AI Assistant, b2. Retrieving passages to hand to a large language model, and when embeddings beat word matching, lives in RAG, b4.

Real world The search that "proved" nothing was wrong. A product team searches the support inbox for a payment partner's name, finds almost nothing and reports the issue as rare. The index had been built from ticket subject lines only, and customers named the partner in the body. Before anyone quotes a search count, the person who built the index should say what text went into it and what was cut.

Part 2 · covers k-means themes, seed instability, NMF and LDA

Themes are a view, not a truth

5 min live

Ask k-means for six groups and it will always return six groups, each with a tidy list of top words that looks like a finding. Change only the random starting point and some of those groups split, merge or swap members. Before a theme gets a name on a slide, check how much of it survives a second seed.

LiveHow the bench makes a theme1 min▶

Start from the TF-IDF table of session 2 with stop words and xxxx out, the light stemmer on, and only words that appear in at least 5 complaints: 1,365 features. Every complaint vector is scaled to length 1. Spherical k-means then picks k complaints at random as starting centres, assigns every complaint to the centre with the highest cosine, moves each centre to the average of its members, and repeats 20 times. A theme is one final group; its "top words" are the heaviest words in its centre.

The random pick in step one is the seed. Nothing else in the recipe is random, and nothing in the recipe guarantees that a different start ends in the same place.

LiveRead seed 1's six themes2 min▶

At k 6 and seed 1 the bench returns six themes of 722, 522, 333, 214, 489 and 720 complaints. Four of them tell you something different about text:

  • 722 · [report credit information item account theft]: the credit reporting dispute, 75% of its members from that queue. A theme that mostly re-finds a queue.
  • 522 · [call car well fargo time vehicle]: "well fargo" is the light stemmer turning "Wells" into "well", the artefact from session 1, now printed as a theme label. Theme words inherit every normalisation choice.
  • 214 · [right u section violat consumer privacy]: a legal-template theme. "u" and "s" are what is left of "U.S.C." in citations of the Fair Credit Reporting Act. It groups complaints by how they are written, not by what went wrong.
  • 489 · [payment loan late student month account]: half its members (purity 0.50) are student loan complaints. Part 3 follows this one month by month.

"Purity" is the share of a theme's complaints that come from its largest queue. Read it with care: the sample is rebalanced (credit reporting is 30% here and 76.7% of the real 2023 rows), so every purity on the bench would move on the real mix.

LiveSame data, another seed: the agreement table2 min▶

Pair agreement takes every pair of complaints and asks: of the pairs that either run put together, what share did both runs put together? 1 means the same groups; values near 0 mean unrelated groupings. The widget compares each seed with seed 1 at the same k:

  • k 4: seed 2 0.487 · seed 3 0.355
  • k 6: seed 2 0.546 · seed 3 0.496
  • k 8: seed 2 0.487 · seed 3 0.387

No setting comes close to 1. At k 8, seed 1 separates a student loan theme [loan student payment mohela plan interest] (318 complaints, purity 0.63) and an identity theft theme [credit report item information theft identity] (481, purity 0.84), so a larger k found sharper groups, and still did not make them stable. The honest sentence for a slide is "one reasonable grouping of these complaints", followed by the agreement value.

Self-studyNMF and LDA: two other ways to get themes (described, not run here)3 min▶

k-means gives each complaint exactly one theme. Two widely used methods let a complaint belong to several themes at once. The bench runs neither; they are described so you recognise them in a vendor's tool or a colleague's notebook.

Non-negative matrix factorisation (NMF). Lee and Seung factor the document-word table into two tables with no negative entries, which "allow[s] only additive, not subtractive, combinations"; on text it learns "semantic features of text" (Nature 401: 788-791, 1999). Each complaint becomes a weighted sum of parts, read the way you read a k-means centre.

Latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA). Blei, Ng and Jordan describe "a generative probabilistic model for collections of discrete data such as text corpora" (JMLR 3: 993-1022, 2003): each document is a mixture of topics and each topic is a distribution over words.

Both start from random initial values too. Whatever method you choose, the check is the same as tonight's: re-run with other seeds and report how much survives.

Real world The theme that vanished between two decks. An insights team presents eight "customer pain themes" in March. In June someone re-runs the notebook on the same data, gets a different random start, and "billing confusion" is gone, absorbed into two other themes. Leadership asks what changed in the business. Nothing did. Fixing the seed hides the problem; reporting the agreement across seeds shows it.

Part 3 · covers volume drift, theme drift and label schema change

Drift: the month the student loans came back

5 min live

A theme is most useful when you count it over time. Split the 300 student loan complaints in the sample by the month they were received and a shape appears that nobody needed a model to find, lined up with two dates in the US Department of Education's own report.

LiveVolume drift: count one queue by month2 min▶

Student loan complaints per month in the sample, January to December 2023: 17, 14, 10, 5, 6, 7, 17, 24, 49, 67, 47, 37. From 5 in April to 67 in October.

Read the level with care. The sample took a fixed quota of 300 student loan complaints for the year, drawn evenly from the queue's whole year, so the monthly shape follows the real data but the level is set by the quota, not by how many complaints CFPB received. "67 in October" is a share of a fixed draw, not a national count.

The US Department of Education's Federal Student Aid office records that "student loan interest would resume on September 1, 2023, and payments resumed in October 2023" (FY2024 Annual Report, p. 45). The two dashed lines in the figure sit exactly there.

LiveTheme drift: 18 in June, 79 in October1 min▶

The [payment loan late student month account] theme from Part 2 (k 6, seed 1) counted by month: 35, 34, 35, 23, 21, 18, 32, 46, 64, 79, 61, 41. It more than quadruples from June to October. The theme was found without any queue labels, yet it rises with the student loan queue. That is what a theme watchlist is for: a group you can count every month, whether or not it matches a product line.

It is also why Part 2's warning matters here. With a different seed this theme might be split or merged, and the monthly line would change with it. Track a theme only after it has survived a seed check, or track a saved query whose definition cannot move.

Live"Coincides with" is not "caused by"1 min▶

The rise lines up with the dates. It does not tell you why. Other things could move student loan complaints in the same months: servicer changes, news coverage, a campaign encouraging people to complain, a change in how CFPB categorised them. The data on this page cannot separate them. The defensible sentence is: "In this sample, student loan complaints rose from 5 in April to 67 in October, which coincides with the resumption of interest (1 September 2023) and payments (October 2023)." To say more, read the complaints themselves and name what they describe.

Self-studyLabel drift: when the categories rename themselves2 min▶

Text drifts, and so do the labels attached to it. In August 2023 the CFPB product "Credit card or prepaid card" stops and two new products, "Credit card" and "Prepaid card", begin; the same month "Credit reporting, credit repair services, or other personal consumer reports" becomes "Credit reporting or other personal consumer reports". The sample builder maps both old and new names to one queue, which is recorded on Ledgerly's corpus card in session 1.

Without that mapping, a monthly chart of "Credit card" complaints would show a category appearing from nothing in August, and a classifier trained on January's labels would have no answer for "Prepaid card". Check the label list every time you refresh the data, and keep the mapping next to the watchlist.

Real world The spike that was a dropdown. A bank's complaints dashboard shows "fees" complaints doubling in one month and an incident review is opened. The cause was a change to the intake form: a new "fees and charges" option was added, and agents started choosing it instead of "other". Before you explain a monthly jump with the world, check whether the way the data is recorded changed that month.

Rebuild it in Python. This runs on the 2023 rows of the shard from session 1. It draws a plain random sample rather than the bench's quota sample, uses scikit-learn's ordinary (Euclidean) k-means on length-normalised TF-IDF rows, which behaves close to spherical k-means, and measures agreement with the adjusted Rand index, a different scale from the widget's pair agreement. Expect different themes and different values; the instability is the thing to reproduce.

Ranking: BM25, k1 1.2, b 0.75, Lucene idf. Cosine on TF-IDF kept as a second opinion. Why these values: defaults disagree (IIR: k1 1.2 to 2, b 0.75; Robertson and Zaragoza: b 0.5 with k1 2 is common). Change only after checking 20 queries with known answers. Known gaps: "zelle" is not in the index (50-word cut). Templates tie at the top ("identity theft inquiry": 5 copies at 11.825); deduplicate before counting. THEME WATCHLIST (thresholds and owners are choices, not findings) 1. Theme [payment loan late student] (k 6, seed 1; agreement 0.546 with seed 2) Alert: a month above 2x its trailing 6-month median. Owner: student loan lead. 2. Query "identity theft inquiry" Alert: 10+ complaints with identical opening words in one month. Owner: credit reporting lead. 3. Query "repossessed my car" Alert: a month up 50% on the previous month. Owner: auto lending lead. 4. Term "zelle" (needs a full-text index first; the 50-word index cannot see it) Alert: any month above 5 complaints. Owner: deposits lead. ROUTINE Monthly: count every item on the same index; read 10 complaints behind any alert. Quarterly: re-run themes with seeds 1, 2, 3; record the agreement; re-read the product list (Aug 2023 renames mapped on page 1). A rise "coincides with" a date; it is not a cause. Owner: ____________ Date: ____________

A watchlist item without an owner is a chart nobody reads. The threshold and the name matter more than the method behind the count. A saved BM25 query with a named owner will catch a rising issue sooner than a beautiful theme map that nobody is responsible for opening.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · In BM25, what does the parameter b control?

b scales length normalisation: b = 1 is full normalisation by length, b = 0 ignores length. Rarity is the idf's job and saturation of repeats is k1's. IIR suggests b = 0.75; Robertson and Zaragoza report b = 0.5 as a common choice, so write down which you used.

2 · Seed 1 and seed 2 at k 6 have a pair agreement of 0.546. What does that tell you?

Pair agreement counts pairs together in both runs over pairs together in either. There is no ground truth for themes, so it is not accuracy, and fixing seed 1 only hides the instability. No setting on the bench gets near 1 (0.355 to 0.546).

3 · Student loan complaints in the sample rose from 5 in April to 67 in October, right after interest and payments resumed. Why is that not evidence that the resumption caused the rise?

The FSA dates are read at source and the shape of the rise is real. What the counts cannot do is rule out other changes in the same months, including changes in how complaints were recorded. "Coincides with" is the honest verb; reading the complaints is how you learn more.
