Part 1 · covers cost per thousand documents, volume and latency

The bill is the smallest cost

5 min live

Teams argue about the price per million tokens because it is the one cost with a number on it. Work it out properly and it is usually small. The costs that decide the question are the ones without a price tag: who maintains the thing, whether tomorrow's labels match today's, how fast an answer comes back, and where the text goes.

LiveThe bill, worked out from the bench2 min▶

The bench's modelled rung prices one realistic set-up: every held-out complaint sent at its full length (not the 50-word cut the classical rungs see), with a 120-token instruction naming the six queues, and a 4-token answer. It assumes 1.3 tokens per English word. The 600 complaints are 115,069 words, so 221,590 input tokens and 2,400 output tokens.

At placeholder prices of $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output tokens, that is $0.39 per 1,000 complaints. Scale it to a constructed Ledgerly volume of 30,000 complaints a month: 0.3893 x 30 = about $11.68 a month. Every one of those prices is a placeholder, not a quote: re-verify the vendor's current list before the number goes into any note, because prices change.

The point is not that $11.68 is cheap or dear. It is that the bill is rarely what decides this.

LiveSpeed: milliseconds against a network call2 min▶

Every classical rung on the bench trains on 2,400 complaints and predicts 600 more inside your browser tab. The bench prints the time on every run, next to "Train + predict"; read your own figure tonight, because it depends on your laptop. It is measured in milliseconds, and nothing leaves the page.

An API rung is a network round trip per complaint (or per batch), with a queue in front of it. That brings two operational costs that never appear on the invoice. Latency: if routing happens while the customer waits, the answer has to come back in time, every time. Rate limits: vendors cap requests and tokens per minute, so a month-end spike or a backfill of last year's complaints becomes a scheduling problem. Neither is a reason to refuse an LLM; both belong in the note with a plan.

Self-studyWhat the price leaves out3 min▶

Prompt maintenance. The instruction that names the six queues is code. When a queue is renamed or split, someone rewrites it, and every rewrite changes labels on complaints nobody looked at. Test a new prompt the way you would test a new model: on held-out labelled rows, before it goes live.

Version pinning. A hosted model behind a moving alias can change under you. Pin an exact model version where the vendor allows it, record it with every label, and plan for the day that version is retired.

Data leaving the building. Ledgerly's own complaints carry names and account details before any scrubbing (the CFPB redacts them as XXXX before publishing; your inbox does not). Sending them to a vendor is a contract, privacy and retention question that has to be answered before the first call, not after.

And the classical side is not free. R5 needed 2,400 labelled complaints, someone who reads its error table, and retraining when the labels or the vocabulary move. Write both columns honestly or the note is advocacy.

Real world The cheap pilot that stalled. A support team prices LLM tagging for its tickets, finds the monthly bill smaller than one software licence, and gets a yes in a week. The pilot then sits for three months: legal needs to review where ticket text is stored, the security team asks which model version produced last month's labels, and a backlog import hits the vendor's rate limit on day one. None of those costs were in the spreadsheet; all of them were predictable.

Part 2 · covers the contested comparison of zero-shot LLMs and trained classifiers

The evidence disagrees, and why both sides can be right

5 min live

Ask "are LLMs better at labelling text?" and you will find a peer-reviewed yes and several careful no's. Do not pick the paper that agrees with your manager. Read what each study compared against, because that is where the disagreement comes from, and then measure on your own data.

LiveThe case for: ahead of crowd workers, at a fraction of the price2 min▶

Gilardi, Alizadeh and Kubli gave ChatGPT the same annotation tasks as crowd workers on 6,183 tweets and news articles. Zero-shot, its accuracy "exceeds that of crowd workers by about 25 percentage points on average", at a per-annotation cost under $0.003, about thirty times cheaper than MTurk (Gilardi, Alizadeh and Kubli (2023), PNAS 120(30): e2305016120).

One detail worth knowing before you quote it: the arXiv preprint said twenty times cheaper and 2,382 tweets. The published PNAS version is the one to cite. The baseline is the important part: the comparison is against paid crowd workers, the usual way research teams got labels at scale.

LiveThe case against: behind models trained for the task2 min▶

Bucher and Martini compared fine-tuned smaller models with GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and Claude Opus prompted zero-shot on text classification. The fine-tuned models "consistently and significantly outperform larger, zero-shot prompted models", and fine-tuning wins in all cases they tested (Bucher and Martini (2024), arXiv:2406.08660). The abstract gives no single headline number.

Kocon and colleagues ran ChatGPT on 25 tasks against the best available models: "the average loss in quality of the ChatGPT model was about 25%" for zero-shot and few-shot evaluation (Kocon et al. (2023), arXiv:2302.10724).

Ziems and colleagues tested 13 models on 25 computational social science benchmarks. On classification, LLMs "fail to outperform the best fine-tuned models"; they argue LLMs are most useful as collaborators alongside people, and found their written explanations often better than the crowd workers' reference explanations (Ziems et al. (2024), Computational Linguistics (arXiv:2305.03514)).

LiveWhy both sides can be right at once1 min▶

Different baselines. Beating crowd workers and losing to a fine-tuned model are not contradictory; crowd labels and a task-trained model are different bars. Your R5 at 0.790 is a task-trained model, not a crowd worker.

Different tasks. Stance in political tweets is not complaint routing. A task where the categories need world knowledge favours a model that has read the world; a task with stable product vocabulary favours a model that has read your labels.

Different model generations. These studies span 2023 to 2024 and several model versions. Results for one generation are evidence about that generation. None of them measured Ledgerly's six queues, and neither did the bench: that is the gap the capstone closes.

Self-studyWhat the bench adds, and what it leaves for you2 min▶

The bench measured six classical rungs on 600 held-out real complaints and printed the cost and audit columns for an LLM rung. It did not measure LLM accuracy: no complaint was sent anywhere. That is a deliberate boundary, not an omission to paper over. Anyone who quotes "the LLM scored X on our bench" from this work is quoting something that does not exist.

Remember the rebalanced sample whenever you compare accuracies: credit reporting is 30% of the bench's sample and 76.7% of the real 2023 rows. An LLM check on Ledgerly's own inbox will have Ledgerly's own mix, so compare both models on the same rows, never one model's bench number against another's vendor number.

Real world Two slides, two papers, one meeting. In a steering meeting one team shows a paper where an LLM beats human annotators; the other shows a paper where trained models beat the LLM every time. The meeting ends without a decision because both slides are accurate. The way out is not a third paper. It is two hundred of the company's own documents, labelled by its own people, with both approaches scored on the same rows.

Part 3 · covers reproducibility, drift and governance

Audit, repeatability and what you can defend

5 min live

A routing decision that affects a customer has to be explainable later: which model labelled this complaint, with what settings, and would it do the same again? A seeded, versioned classical pipeline answers that by construction. A hosted model can answer it too, but only if you build the logging deliberately.

LivePin it, log it, replay it2 min▶

Everything on the bench is fixed: the sample was drawn with seed 2023, the shuffle trap uses seed 13, the logistic model's training order is seeded, and the sample itself is a file you can hash. Run R5 tomorrow and you get 0.790 again, 474 of 600. That is what lets the bench number go into a note.

A hosted model can be made auditable, but it takes deliberate work. Pin the exact model version, the full prompt text and every generation setting. Log every label with the complaint id, the version, a hash of the prompt and the time. Replay a fixed set of labelled complaints on a schedule and on every vendor change, and compare. Even with all three, two runs of the same model may not agree on every complaint; measure how often they do, and put the figure in the note.

LiveLabels and data drift too2 min▶

The label schema moved mid-year. In August 2023 the CFPB's product "Credit card or prepaid card" stops and "Credit card" and "Prepaid card" begin, and the long credit reporting product name is shortened the same month. Ledgerly's sample maps both versions to one queue, and the corpus card records it. A label schema is part of what you audit: a classifier trained before the change, or a prompt written before it, is answering a slightly different question afterwards.

The data can disappear. The CFPB's current field list ("How we share complaint data", modified Sept. 14, 2026) has no consumer complaint narrative field, and neither does today's export. Every narrative on the bench comes from a February 2024 snapshot. Text you depend on but do not own is a data dependency with no service agreement; the same is true of a hosted model you do not control.

LiveWhen the big model earns its place, and when it does not2 min▶

The big model tends to earn its place when:

  • the categories are open-ended or not in your training labels yet (a new complaint type, "anything about a scam");
  • there is very little labelled data and no budget to make more;
  • the job is explanation or extraction (summarise, pull the amount and date, say why) rather than routing to a fixed queue;
  • volume is low enough that a person can review what comes out.

The classical model tends to win when:

  • the labels are stable and already exist;
  • volume is high and the answer is needed fast;
  • audit demands the same label on replay, years later;
  • it already meets the bar: R5 at 0.790 on six fixed queues, with its weakest queue (debt collection recall 0.608) named and understood.

Neither list is a verdict. "Tends to" means: check it on your own rows.

Self-studyEmbeddings and retrieval belong elsewhere1 min▶

Between a bag of words and a full LLM call sits a middle rung: dense embeddings, where each complaint becomes a vector that captures meaning beyond shared words, fed to a small classifier or used to retrieve similar cases for an LLM. That rung is taught properly in RAG, embeddings (b1), and retrieval for an LLM in RAG, retrieval basics (b4). Treat it as a fifth option for the note, and score it on the same 200 rows.

Real world The quarter nobody could reproduce. An operations team tags customer emails with a hosted model through an unpinned alias. In the next quarter the share tagged "billing dispute" shifts noticeably, and the board asks whether disputes really rose. Nobody can say: the vendor updated the model in between, and the team logged labels but not versions. The fix took an afternoon (pin, log, replay a fixed labelled set monthly); the missing quarter could not be recovered.

Design the 200-complaint check before anyone buys. Take 200 recent complaints from your own inbox, spread across all six queues, and have two people label them without seeing any model's answer; settle their disagreements and keep that as the reference. Run R5 and the pinned LLM set-up on the same 200 rows, and run the LLM twice to see how often it agrees with itself. With 200 rows, one complaint is half a percentage point of accuracy, so treat small gaps as noise.

★ Step 3 · Python, scoring the check (pandas + scikit-learn)import pandas as pd from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, f1_score # one row per complaint: id, gold (agreed human label), # r5_label, llm_run1, llm_run2 (same pinned model, prompt and settings) df = pd.read_csv("ledgerly_check_200.csv") for col in ["r5_label", "llm_run1"]: acc = accuracy_score(df["gold"], df[col]) mf1 = f1_score(df["gold"], df[col], average="macro") print(col, "accuracy", round(acc, 3), "macro F1", round(mf1, 3)) print("LLM agrees with itself:", round((df["llm_run1"] == df["llm_run2"]).mean(), 3)) print(pd.crosstab(df["gold"], df["llm_run1"])) # your numbers will differ: this is your own labelled sample, not the bench

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Gilardi et al. find zero-shot ChatGPT about 25 points ahead; Bucher and Martini find fine-tuned smaller models ahead in all cases. How can both be right?

Beating crowd workers and losing to a task-trained model are different claims about different bars. Neither paper measured your queues, which is why the note calls for a check on your own rows.

2 · The bench's LLM rung is "modelled, not measured". What does that oblige you to do?

The cost is a calculation with placeholder prices and stated assumptions; the accuracy was never computed. Both gaps are closed by you: a dated price check and a labelled check on the same rows as R5.

3 · Which situation most favours using an LLM?

Open-ended categories, no labelled data and low volume a person can review are where a large model earns its place. Fixed queues, high volume, plenty of labels and strict replay favour the classical model.
