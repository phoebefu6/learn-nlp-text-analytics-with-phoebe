Part 1 · covers counts and the sparse vector

Raw counts let the common words win

5 min live

The simplest cell value is how many times the word appears in the complaint. Sum those counts over a queue, sort, and you expect to see what each queue is about. You see grammar instead. Here are the six queues side by side.

LiveRaw counts: honest, loud and the same everywhere2 min▶

Sum the counts over all complaints in a queue, keep stop words and redaction marks, and every one of the six queues has the same top seven: xxxx, i, the, to, my, a, and, in varying order. That is Zipf from session 1 showing up again: the head of the distribution is grammar and redaction, and it is the same head for credit reporting and for car loans.

A count answers "how much of this complaint is this word?". It does not answer "how unusual is it that this complaint uses this word?". For routing, the second question is the one that carries signal. "Debt" in a complaint is news; "the" never is.

LiveThe sparse vector: 5,250 cells, at most 50 filled2 min▶

Each complaint is now a vector: one number per vocabulary word, 5,250 numbers long. Because each complaint keeps at most 50 tokens, at most 50 of those numbers are non-zero; more than 99% are zero. Libraries store only the non-zero cells (a sparse matrix), which is why a 3,000 by 5,250 table fits easily in memory.

Every weighting in this session changes the values of the non-zero cells and never turns a zero into a non-zero. A word the complaint did not use stays at zero under counts, TF-IDF and every variant. Weighting decides how loud each present word is, not which words are present.

Self-studyBinary or count: does saying "late" three times matter?2 min▶

A binary cell records only presence: 1 if the word appears, 0 if not. A count cell records how often. For short, focused texts the two often behave alike. For longer narratives, repetition can mean emphasis ("late, late, late again") or just a writing habit, and counts let one repeated word dominate a row.

There is a middle road you will meet in Part 2: grow the cell with the logarithm of the count, so the second "late" adds less than the first. Manning, Raghavan and Schutze give the same family of choices for term weighting (Introduction to Information Retrieval, 2008, section 6.2.2). None is right in general; the bench in session 4 is where Ledgerly measures it.

Real world The word cloud that never changed. A service team publishes a weekly "top words in customer complaints" chart. Every week it says "account, the, i, my, called". Leadership stops reading it within a month. Nothing was wrong with the counting; the chart answered "what is common?" when the question was "what is new or distinctive this week?". That second question needs a weighting that knows what is common everywhere.

Part 2 · covers tf, idf, l2 normalisation and two conventions

TF-IDF: rare across documents, frequent in this one

6 min live

TF-IDF multiplies two numbers. Term frequency (tf) says how much this complaint uses the word. Inverse document frequency (idf) says how rare the word is across all complaints. A word scores high only when it is both. The curve below is the whole idea: the more complaints a word appears in, the less each appearance is worth.

LiveThe smooth idf, on Ledgerly's real words2 min▶

Document frequency (df) is the number of complaints that contain the word at least once. scikit-learn's default idf is ln((1+n)/(1+df)) + 1, where n is the number of documents (the guide writes "log"; the code uses the natural log). The two "+1" inside the fraction act as if one extra document contained every word, so nothing divides by zero; the "+1" outside keeps a word that appears everywhere from being zeroed out entirely.

Read the table as a price list. One "scam" is worth more than five appearances of "the". "Mohela" (a federal student loan servicer) is rare overall and concentrated in one queue, which is exactly what a routing feature should be.

LiveThen divide by the row's length (l2 normalisation)2 min▶

After multiplying each count by its idf, scikit-learn divides the whole row by its length: the square root of the sum of squared values. The result is a row whose length is exactly 1. Its defaults, from the TfidfTransformer documentation, are norm='l2', use_idf=True, smooth_idf=True, sublinear_tf=False.

Normalising makes a long complaint and a short complaint comparable: a narrative that says "card" twice in 20 words should not lose to one that says it three times in 50. It also shrinks every value below 1, which matters for some models. Hold that thought: in session 4, TF-IDF with naive Bayes scores 0.608 on the holdout while plain counts with naive Bayes score 0.762.

LiveWhy TF-IDF still leads with xxxx, i and the1 min▶

Switch the widget in the build-along to TF-IDF on raw tokens and the top of each queue is still xxxx, i, the. That surprises people. idf pushes those words down per appearance, but each complaint uses them many times, and the smooth idf never goes below 1. A small multiplier on a big count still beats a big multiplier on a count of one or zero. TF-IDF demotes the common words; it does not delete them. Removing them is a separate decision (stop words, redaction marks), and Part 3 makes it.

Self-studyTwo conventions: scikit-learn's and the textbook's3 min▶

Jurafsky and Martin's current draft defines tf as 1 + log10(count) for a word that appears (0 otherwise) and idf as log10(N/df) (Speech and Language Processing, 2026 draft, 3rd ed., ch 11, equations 11.4 to 11.6). A footnote notes that log10(count+1) was "used in earlier editions". Manning, Raghavan and Schutze define tf-idf weighting in Introduction to Information Retrieval (2008), section 6.2.2.

The two conventions disagree at the edges. Under the textbook idf, a word in every document gets idf 0 and vanishes; under scikit-learn's smooth idf it gets about 1 and stays. Worked example on Ledgerly's counts: the textbook idf of "the" is log10(3,000 / 2,331) = 0.110, of "credit" log10(3,000 / 1,218) = 0.391, of "scam" log10(3,000 / 11) = 2.436. The ranking of words is the same; the size of the gap between common and rare is not.

scikit-learn can use the textbook's tf idea too: sublinear_tf=True replaces a count with 1 + log(count), so the third "late" adds less than the first. It is off by default. Whichever convention you use, write it on the playbook page: two teams quoting "the TF-IDF score" for the same complaint can be quoting different numbers.

Self-studyCosine: the comparison TF-IDF was made for2 min▶

Once every row has length 1, the dot product of two rows is their cosine similarity: 1 when they use the same words in the same proportions, 0 when they share no words. Jurafsky and Martin put it plainly: "By far the most common similarity metric is the cosine" (2026 draft, ch 5). TF-IDF plus cosine is the classic way to ask "which complaints look like this one?", and session 5 compares it with BM25 for search.

Because idf has already turned down the common words, two complaints are judged similar for sharing "repossessed" and "dealer", not for sharing "the" and "my". That is the reason to weight before you compare.

Self-studyTF-IDF elsewhere: item profiles for recommendation1 min▶

The same weighting describes products and articles in a content-based recommender, where each item becomes a TF-IDF profile and users are matched to items by cosine. That use, and how a user profile is built from items, is taught in Recommendation, session b5: content-based filtering. Here TF-IDF is a document weighting for classification, search and themes.

Real world The "similar tickets" button that matched greetings. A help desk adds "show similar tickets" using raw word overlap. Every ticket that opens with "Hi team, I hope you are well" matches every other one. Switching the vectors to TF-IDF with cosine, and nothing else, makes the matches about the problem described rather than the politeness around it.

Part 3 · covers n-grams, minimum document frequency and vocabulary size

Pairs of words, and how big the vocabulary should be

4 min live

The bag of words threw away order. Bigrams (adjacent pairs of words, written with an underscore here) buy a little of it back: "credit report" is one thing, "credit" and "report" are two. The price is a vocabulary that explodes, and the cure is a frequency floor. Here is the bill, in features.

LiveBigrams: credit_report is not credit plus report2 min▶

An n-gram is a run of n adjacent tokens. Unigrams are single words; bigrams are pairs. With stop words and xxxx removed and bigrams added, the top TF-IDF terms of the six queues gain pairs like credit_report (credit reporting and debt collection), credit_card (credit card) and checking_account (checking or savings). These pairs mean more than their parts: "credit" alone appears in complaints about cards, collections and reports.

Remove stop words before forming pairs and the pairs skip over them: "my credit report" becomes credit_report. That is usually what you want for routing and occasionally what you do not ("not authorised" loses its "not" if "not" is on the stop list).

LiveMinimum document frequency: the cheapest cut you will make2 min▶

min_df=2 keeps only features that appear in at least two complaints. On single words it cuts 5,250 columns to 3,054. On the unigram plus bigram table with stop words and xxxx out, it cuts 42,055 to 9,033: 79% of the columns gone. Pairs follow Zipf even harder than words do, so most pairs occur exactly once.

A feature that appears in one complaint cannot help a model generalise: it can only memorise that one complaint. The cost is real but small: every rare product name and every one-off phrase goes too. Write the floor on the playbook page, because a new product name will sit below it until enough complaints mention it.

Self-studyDid bigrams help? It depends on the model2 min▶

Session 4's tuning grid, scored on a validation split taken from the training rows, gives a mixed answer. For naive Bayes on counts, bigrams did not help: alpha 1 scored 0.768 with unigrams and 0.748 with unigrams plus bigrams; alpha 0.3 scored 0.760 and 0.735. The logistic model with TF-IDF and bigrams (min df 2) reached 0.778 on the 600-row holdout, the best untuned rung.

Read those numbers with the sample in mind: it is rebalanced (credit reporting is 30% of the sample, 76.7% of the real 2023 rows), so the accuracies describe this six-queue mix, not Ledgerly's live traffic. Session 4 runs the full comparison; tonight's lesson is only that "more features" is a hypothesis to test, not a default.

Real world The model that memorised reference numbers. A team classifies support emails with every bigram kept. Accuracy on the training emails is superb and drops on next month's mail: the model had learned pairs like "ref 4471" that each appeared in one thread. A minimum document frequency of two, plus a rule that strips numbers, removed the memorised pairs and most of the gap.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · "the" appears in 2,331 of the 3,000 complaints and its smooth idf is 1.252. Why is it close to 1 and not 0?

ln(3,001 / 2,332) is about 0.252, and scikit-learn adds 1. The textbook idf, log10(N/df), would give "the" 0.110 and a word in every complaint exactly 0. Two conventions, two numbers: write yours down.

2 · With stop words and xxxx removed, unigrams plus bigrams make 42,055 features at min df 1 and 9,033 at min df 2. Why does one complaint of extra support cut 79%?

The tail of the bigram distribution is enormous: most pairs are one-offs. A feature seen in one complaint can only memorise that complaint, so the floor costs little signal and most of the columns. Useful pairs like credit_report survive easily.

3 · TF-IDF clearly separates the six queues' top words. Yet on the bench, TF-IDF + naive Bayes scores 0.608 while counts + naive Bayes scores 0.762. What explains it?

Session 4 shows 175 of 180 credit reporting complaints right under R3 while vehicle loan recall falls to 0.283. Credit reporting is 30% of this rebalanced sample (76.7% of the real 2023 rows), so leaning on the prior hides the damage. Logistic regression with the same TF-IDF features reaches 0.778.
