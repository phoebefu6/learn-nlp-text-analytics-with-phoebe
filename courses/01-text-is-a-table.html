Part 0

Where we start

One question runs through all six sessions: what can be counted, classified and searched cheaply, auditably and reproducibly, and where does a large language model actually earn its cost? We answer it on real text. The complaints are 3,000 narratives that US consumers sent to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) in 2023 and agreed to publish. The team reading them is fictional: the Constructed Ledgerly example, a consumer-lending fintech whose complaints team must route every complaint to one of six product queues and spot rising issues before they become a regulator's question. Each session adds one page to Ledgerly's text playbook.

Part 1 · covers documents, tokens and the bag of words

From a complaint to a row

5 min live

A model cannot read a sentence. It can read a row of numbers. Text analytics starts by deciding what one row is (a document), what the columns are (the vocabulary) and what goes in each cell (a count, for now). Follow one real complaint through the three steps and notice the moment the word order disappears.

LiveDocument, token, vocabulary: three words you will use all night3 min▶

A document is whatever one row stands for. For Ledgerly it is one complaint narrative. It could have been one sentence, one email thread or one customer's whole history; the choice decides what every later number means, so write it down.

A token is one unit the text is cut into. Here a token is a run of letters: the export lowercased everything, removed apostrophes ("I've" became "ive"), and dropped numbers, money amounts and punctuation. Those are already three decisions, made before you touched the data.

The vocabulary is the set of distinct tokens across all documents. In Ledgerly's 3,000 complaints it is 5,250 words, so the table has 5,250 columns. Each complaint keeps at most 50 tokens, which means at most 50 non-zero cells: more than 99% of every row is zero. That is why text tables are stored as sparse matrices, which keep only the non-zero cells.

Self-studyThe bag of words, and what order costs you4 min▶

Jurafsky and Martin describe the move exactly: represent a text document "as if it were a bag of words", an unordered set that keeps each word's count and nothing about its position (Speech and Language Processing, 2024 draft, chapter 4, section 4.1). "My card was not charged" and "not my card, I was charged" become nearly the same row.

That sounds fatal and mostly is not. For routing a complaint to a product queue, the words "repossessed", "dealer" and "vehicle" carry the signal whatever order they arrive in. Order matters far more for questions like "was the consumer satisfied?", where negation flips the meaning. Session 2 buys a little order back with two-word tokens (bigrams); session 6 asks when you need a model that reads order properly.

Self-studyReading model output is a different job2 min▶

If your role is to read a vendor's sentiment or topic dashboard rather than build one, the consumer's side of that skill (what to ask, what a score can and cannot mean) lives in Data Literacy, session 7. These six sessions take the builder's side: you will make the table, the model and the checks yourself.

Real world The text column that disappeared. The CFPB's Consumer Complaint Database held 19,251,022 complaints on 19 November 2026, under a CC0 public-domain dedication. Its current field list has 15 fields, and "Consumer complaint narrative" is not one of them: the live export, the API and the bulk CSV carry no narratives. The 3,000 narratives here come from a February 2024 snapshot of the same public data. If your analysis depends on text you do not own, record the snapshot date on day one; the source can change under you.

Part 2 · covers case, stop words, stemming and redaction marks

Every normalisation throws something away

6 min live

Normalising means making different spellings count as the same column. It shrinks the table and concentrates the signal, and every filter also deletes something that was true about the text. Watch three filters run over the whole corpus, then switch them on yourself.

LiveStop words: half the tokens, almost none of the meaning (usually)2 min▶

A stop list is a fixed set of very common words dropped before counting. The bench uses a 126-word English list. Removing it deletes 64,885 of 141,198 tokens across the corpus, 46% of everything, and only 119 of the 5,250 columns: the most frequent words are few and enormous.

"Usually" matters. The scikit-learn documentation warns that "there are several known issues in our provided 'english' stop word list", and that popular stop lists "may include words that are highly informative to some tasks". For complaints, "not" and "no" are on most lists and carry the whole meaning of "I did not authorise this". Session 4 tests stop-word removal on the routing task: for the logistic model it never helped.

LiveStemming: merging the right words and the wrong ones2 min▶

A stemmer cuts suffixes so that "report", "reports", "reported" and "reporting" land in one column. The bench uses a deliberately light suffix stripper, not the Porter algorithm (Porter 1980, Program 14(3): 130-137), so you can see every cut it makes. It takes the vocabulary from 5,250 to 4,159 words.

On this corpus it also makes three kinds of mistake you can spot in the widget below: it turns "dated" into "dat" and "received" into "receiv" (harmless, the columns are still consistent); it splits a family ("charged" becomes "charg", "charge" stays "charge"); and it damages names ("Wells", as in Wells Fargo, becomes "well"). Session 5's themes will show that last one surfacing as a theme label.

LiveRedaction marks are the most frequent word1 min▶

CFPB scrubs personal details before publishing and replaces them with runs of X: names, account numbers and dates become XXXX and XX/XX/XXXX. The export turns every run into one token, xxxx. It is the single most frequent token in the corpus (11,461 times, 8.1% of all tokens), ahead of "i" and "the". It tells you nothing about the product queue and a lot about how the text was prepared. Keep it, drop it, or count it as a feature in its own right, but decide.

Self-studyCase folding, and what lowercase hides2 min▶

The export lowercased every narrative, so "US" (the country), "us" (the pronoun) and "U.S." (cut into "u" and "s") collide, and "Apple Card" the product becomes two ordinary words. Lowercasing is right far more often than it is wrong for routing, and it is still a decision. If a later task needs names (which bank, which card), keep a cased copy of the text next to the table rather than trying to recover case afterwards.

Real world A filter that deletes the signal. A team classifying "is this a fraud complaint?" switches on a standard stop list and accuracy drops: "not" and "never" were doing the work in "I did not make this purchase" and "I never received the card". The fix is not a cleverer model; it is a shorter stop list, written for the task and stored with the corpus card.

Part 3 · covers Zipf's law on the real corpus

A few words do most of the work

4 min live

Sort the vocabulary by how often each word appears and a strikingly regular shape appears: the second word is about half as frequent as the first, the tenth about a tenth. Manning, Raghavan and Schutze state it as Zipf's law: "the collection frequency cf_i of the i th most common term is proportional to 1/i" (Introduction to Information Retrieval, 2008). Here is the same law in Ledgerly's complaints.

LiveWhat Zipf means for your table2 min▶

The head is noise for routing. The ten most frequent tokens (xxxx, i, the, to, my, a, and, of, on, that) are 31.4% of all text and say nothing about which queue a complaint belongs in. Session 2 shows TF-IDF pushing them down automatically.

The tail is mostly empty columns. 2,094 of the 5,250 words (39.9%) appear exactly once in the whole corpus. Together they are only 2,094 of 141,198 tokens (1.5%), yet they are two columns in every five. A rule like "keep words that appear in at least two documents" cuts the table by roughly two fifths and loses almost no text. It also deletes every rare product name and every typo, which is sometimes exactly the signal you wanted.

The vocabulary never stops growing. Add more complaints and new rare words keep arriving, so a model built today will meet words it has never seen. Every method in the next sessions has to say what it does with them.

Self-studyWhy the slope is -1.11 and not exactly -12 min▶

The slope comes from a least-squares line through log count against log rank for the top 1,000 words. Raw tokens give -1.11; after removing stop words, xxxx and stemming it is -0.98. Zipf's law is an empirical regularity, not a theorem, and its exponent moves with the corpus, the tokeniser and the range of ranks you fit. Manning, Raghavan and Schutze present it as a model of the distribution of terms, good enough to estimate how big an index will be; treat it the same way.

Real world The index that kept growing. A support team builds a keyword dashboard from last quarter's tickets and freezes the vocabulary. Six months later a new product name dominates the tickets and appears nowhere on the dashboard, because it was not in the frozen word list. Zipf predicts it: new words never stop arriving. Refresh the vocabulary on a schedule and log what entered it.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Ledgerly's table has 5,250 columns and each complaint keeps at most 50 tokens. What follows?

At most 50 non-zero cells out of 5,250 is under 1% filled. That is normal for text, and it is why every library stores document-term matrices as sparse matrices.

2 · Removing stop words deletes 46% of all tokens but only 119 of 5,250 columns. Why the difference?

The most frequent words are a tiny set with huge counts. Removing them shrinks the text a lot and the vocabulary barely at all. Rare words do the opposite: two fifths of the columns, 1.5% of the tokens.

3 · The light stemmer turns "Wells" (as in Wells Fargo) into "well". What is the right response?

Every normalisation deletes something true. The right call is empirical and task-specific: session 4 shows the stemmer helping the logistic model slightly, and session 5 shows the same bug labelling a theme "well fargo".
