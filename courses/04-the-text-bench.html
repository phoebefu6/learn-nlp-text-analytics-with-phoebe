Part 1 · covers the six rungs and two numbers per rung

The ladder, rung by rung

6 min live

Every rung is scored twice. Accuracy counts how many of the 600 complaints land in the right queue. Macro F1 averages the F1 of all six queues equally, so a rung that ignores the small queues cannot hide behind the big one. Read the two numbers together, and watch rung 3.

LiveR0 and R1: the floor, and the rules a team writes on day one2 min▶

R0 sends every complaint to credit reporting, the biggest queue in the training rows. Accuracy 0.300 (180 of 600), macro F1 0.077: five queues get nothing. Any model that does not clearly beat this has learned nothing, and on the real, unbalanced 2023 mix (76.7% credit reporting) this rung would score far higher while still being useless. That is why accuracy never travels alone.

R1 is six keyword lists checked in order (student loan words first, then vehicle, collection, banking, "card", credit reporting) with the majority queue as the fallback. Accuracy 0.610, macro F1 0.615. Read its table in the widget: credit reporting recall is 0.922, but its precision is 0.466, because everything the rules miss falls through to it. Card recall is 0.333: plenty of card complaints never say "card" in their first 50 words.

LiveR2 to R5: naive Bayes, the surprising drop, logistic regression, tuning3 min▶

R2, counts and naive Bayes (add-one smoothing, the scikit-learn default alpha of 1.0): 0.762. A few lines of counting and smoothing beat the hand-written rules by 0.152. (The bench to try every rung yourself is in the build-along below.)

R3 swaps counts for TF-IDF and drops to 0.608. Naive Bayes multiplies word probabilities estimated from summed weights. TF-IDF with length normalisation shrinks every value below 1, so the evidence from the words becomes small next to the class prior, and the model falls back on the biggest queue: it gets 175 of 180 credit reporting complaints right and only 0.283 of vehicle loans. A better weighting for one model is a worse one for another; session 2's weights were chosen for comparing documents, not for this model.

R4, TF-IDF with bigrams into logistic regression (ten passes of stochastic gradient descent, fixed seed): 0.778. R5 lets a validation split choose among twelve settings and reaches 0.790, 474 of 600. The last 0.012 is real but small; most of the gain arrived at R2.

Self-studyWhy macro F1, and when to report weighted F1 instead2 min▶

Macro F1 gives the 60 vehicle-loan complaints the same vote as the 180 credit-reporting ones. That matches a routing question where every queue has an owner who must not be starved. If the cost of a mistake really is proportional to volume, a weighted average fits better. Pick the average that matches who pays for errors, write it on the bench page, and keep accuracy beside it. The definitions of precision, recall and F1 are taught in full in Intro to ML, session b7; here we only read them on text.

Real world The 92% classifier. A team reports 92% accuracy on its ticket router and is congratulated. Its biggest category is 90% of tickets. Macro F1 would have shown the small categories at almost zero, and the 2-point gain over "always say the biggest category" would have been the honest headline. On the real 2023 complaint mix, R0 alone would score about 0.77.

Part 2 · covers validation splits and the once-only test set

Tuning without touching the test set

4 min live

Every setting you compare on the held-out rows leaks a little of them into your choice. Do it twelve times and the reported number is partly a selection effect. R5 never looks at the 600 held-out complaints until the very end: it carves its own validation set out of the training rows.

LiveWhat the grid found, and what it did not2 min▶

The grid tries four naive Bayes settings (smoothing 1 or 0.3, with and without bigrams) and eight logistic settings (stop words in or out, stemmer on or off, learning rate 1 or 2). On the 600 validation rows the naive Bayes settings scored 0.735 to 0.768 and the logistic ones 0.767 to 0.787. The winner, row 8, keeps stop words, turns the light stemmer on and uses learning rate 2.

Two findings worth writing on the playbook page. Removing stop words never helped the logistic model here (0.768 to 0.777 with them out, 0.767 to 0.787 with them in): words like "my" and "i" carry a little signal in complaint text, and a learned weight can simply ignore the rest. Bigrams did not help naive Bayes either (0.748 and 0.735 against 0.768 and 0.760). Session 1's "decide the switches on the bench, not by taste" was not a formality.

Self-studyValidation split, cross-validation, and a split by time3 min▶

One validation slice of 600 rows is noisy: the gap between the top logistic settings is a handful of complaints. Five-fold cross-validation (rotate which quarter of the training rows is held back, average the five scores) is steadier and costs five times the compute, which is trivial at this size.

A random split also flatters any text model in one specific way: complaints from the same month share wording, templates and events, so random test rows look like the training rows. Ledgerly will deploy on next month's complaints, so the honest test is a split by time: train on January to September, test on October to December. The homework asks you to run it in Python; expect a lower number, and session 5's drift chart shows why. The general rules for splits are in Intro to ML, session b2.

Real world Twenty experiments, one test set. A team tries twenty feature tweaks, keeps whichever scores best on the test set each time, and ships at 0.83. In production it settles at 0.79. Nothing broke: the test set had quietly become a training set for the choices. A validation split, or a fresh test set at the end, keeps the reported number honest.

Part 3 · covers memorisation, the break button and the modelled LLM rung

Two traps, a break button, and a rung we cannot score

5 min live

A bench earns trust when it can report a worse number. Two switches in the widget make that visible: one scores the model on the complaints it trained on (the trap that makes every classifier look superb), the other shuffles the training labels (the break that must destroy the score if the bench measures anything real).

LiveThe trap: scoring on what the model has seen2 min▶

Scored on its own 2,400 training complaints, R4 reaches 0.975 and R2 0.890. The logistic model with bigrams has about as many weights as there are word pairs in the training text, and it can memorise most of the complaints it was shown. The gap between 0.975 and 0.778 is not skill; it is the size of the illusion.

Text has quieter versions of the same trap, and none of them needs a mistake in the code: the same complaint filed twice (once per company) landing in both train and test; template letters ("under 15 USC 1681 section 602...") copied by many consumers; the label leaking into the text ("this student loan complaint"). Search the training set for near-copies of your test rows before you trust a number.

LiveThe break button: a bench must be able to fail1 min▶

"Shuffle the training labels" keeps every complaint and every queue size and randomly reassigns which complaint has which label (a fixed seed, so everyone sees the same result). The models now learn nothing true. Scored honestly on the 600 held-out complaints, R4 falls to 0.195 and R2 to 0.247, both below the 0.300 majority rung. If a bench still reported 0.7 after this, it would be leaking the answer somewhere.

LiveThe modelled rung: what a large language model would cost here2 min▶

The widget's last panel prices sending each held-out complaint, at full length, to a large language model with an instruction listing the six queues. It is modelled: no complaint is sent anywhere, there is no API key, and its accuracy is not measured on this bench. The assumptions are printed: 1.3 tokens per English word, a 120-token instruction, 4 output tokens. The 600 complaints are 115,069 words, so 221,590 input tokens. At placeholder prices of $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output tokens that is $0.39 per 1,000 complaints. Change the prices to the vendor's current list before quoting any figure; prices move monthly.

Notice what the cheap bill does not settle. The published evidence on accuracy disagrees (Gilardi et al. 2023 found zero-shot labels beating crowd workers; Bucher and Martini 2024 found fine-tuned smaller models winning in every case they tested), and repeatability depends on pinning the model version and prompt. Session 6 weighs all of it.

Real world The vendor demo. A vendor demonstrates a text classifier at 97% on "your data". Ask one question: were those complaints in its training or few-shot examples? If the honest answer is "some of them", you are looking at the left panel of the figure above. Ask for the score on rows it has never seen, and for its break-button result.

Homework

Before session 5

40 min
  1. Split by time. In Python, train R4's recipe on January to September 2023 complaints and test on October to December. Compare with the random split's 0.778 and write one sentence on why they differ.
  2. Hunt for near-duplicates. For each of 50 held-out complaints, find its most similar training complaint by cosine similarity on TF-IDF. How many pairs are near-copies (similarity above 0.8)? What would removing them do to the score?
  3. Cost one misroute. Ask (or estimate, labelled as an estimate) how many minutes a wrongly routed complaint costs your team. Multiply by 210 misroutes per 1,000. That number belongs next to accuracy on page 4.
  4. Price the modelled rung honestly. Find one vendor's current published price per million tokens, enter it, and record the date you checked.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · TF-IDF + naive Bayes (0.608) scores below counts + naive Bayes (0.762). What is the best reading?

R3 gets 175 of 180 credit reporting complaints and only 0.283 of vehicle loans: the prior is winning. The same TF-IDF features feed R4, the logistic model, which reaches 0.778.

2 · Why does R5 choose its setting on a validation slice of the training rows instead of the 600 held-out rows?

Each choice made by looking at the test rows leaks a little of them into the model. The validation number (0.787) chooses; the held-out number (0.790) is reported once.

3 · With shuffled training labels, R4 scores 0.195 on the held-out rows. What does that prove?

A break button is a test of the measuring instrument. Below the 0.300 majority floor is exactly where a model with no true signal should land.
