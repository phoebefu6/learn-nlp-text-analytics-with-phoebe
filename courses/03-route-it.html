Part 0

Where we start

Ledgerly's complaints team (Constructed Ledgerly example) has a playbook with two pages: a corpus card and a weighting choice. Page 3 is the first one with a model on it. The job is routing: 3,000 real CFPB complaints from 2023, 2,400 to learn from and 600 held back, each to be sent to credit reporting, debt collection, credit card, checking or savings, student loan, or vehicle loan or lease. We use two classic models because both can be read: naive Bayes, which multiplies a prior by word evidence, and logistic regression, which learns one weight per word per queue. Then we stop asking "how accurate?" and start asking "where do the misroutes go?".

Part 1 · covers the multinomial naive Bayes rung on text

Naive Bayes: priors times word evidence

5 min live

Naive Bayes asks a simple question for each queue: how common is this queue to begin with, and how typical is each word of this complaint for complaints in this queue? Multiply the answers, compare across the six queues, route to the biggest. It is old, fast and transparent, and on Ledgerly's complaints it gets 0.762 of the held-out rows right.

LivePrior times word evidence, added in log space2 min▶

The prior is how common each queue is before you read a word. Ledgerly's 2,400 training rows split 720 / 480 / 360 / 360 / 240 / 240, so the priors are 0.30 for credit reporting, 0.20 for debt collection, 0.15 each for credit card and checking or savings, and 0.10 each for student loan and vehicle loan.

The word evidence is, for each word in the complaint, how often that word appears in the training complaints of that queue, as a share of all the words in that queue. "Repossessed" is common in vehicle loan complaints and rare everywhere else.

The score for a queue is the prior times the evidence of every word. Fifty small probabilities multiplied together underflow a computer, so every implementation adds logarithms instead: log prior plus the sum of log word likelihoods. The queue with the highest sum wins. That is the whole classifier: one table of word counts per queue and an addition.

LiveAdd-one smoothing: one unseen word must not veto a queue2 min▶

Suppose "zelle" never appeared in any training complaint about checking accounts. Its estimated likelihood for that queue is then zero, and zero times anything is zero: one word would rule out the whole queue, however strong the rest of the evidence. Jurafsky and Martin's fix is add-one (Laplace) smoothing, Equation 4.14 in chapter 4 of the 2024 draft of Speech and Language Processing: add 1 to every word's count in every queue, and add the vocabulary size to the denominator so the shares still sum to one.

In scikit-learn the same knob is alpha on MultinomialNB, and its default is 1.0: add-one. The bench's R2 rung uses alpha 1. Session 4's validation grid also tries alpha 0.3; on Ledgerly's rows it did not beat alpha 1.

Self-studyWhy it works despite a false assumption3 min▶

Multiplying word likelihoods is only correct if every word is independent of every other word once you know the queue. That conditional independence assumption is plainly false for complaints: "credit" and "report" travel together, so naive Bayes counts the same evidence twice and its probabilities come out overconfident.

Routing does not need calibrated probabilities. It needs the right queue to have the highest score, and double counting tends to push all queues' scores in the same direction. On Ledgerly's holdout, R2 (word counts plus naive Bayes) gets 457 of 600 right, 0.762, against 0.300 for always guessing credit reporting.

One warning from the bench: feed naive Bayes TF-IDF weights instead of counts (R3) and accuracy falls to 0.608. The l2-normalised weights are all below 1, so the word evidence shrinks and the prior dominates: 175 of 180 credit reporting complaints are right, while vehicle loan recall collapses to 0.283. A better weighting is not better for every model.

Self-studyWhere the derivation lives1 min▶

Bayes' rule, why the denominator can be dropped, and naive Bayes as a general method for any features are derived step by step in Classification & Regression, c6: naive Bayes. Here it is one rung on a text ladder: what it needs (counts, not weights), what it gets on real complaints, and how to read it.

Real world The baseline that stayed in production. A support team needs ticket triage by Monday. Someone trains naive Bayes on last quarter's labelled tickets in an afternoon as "a baseline until the real model arrives". Two years later it is still running, because it retrains in seconds, anyone can print the words behind a decision, and the fancier candidate never beat it by enough to justify the extra machinery. Start with the model you can explain; make the next one earn its place against it.

Part 2 · covers softmax over six queues, learned weights, and reading them

Logistic regression: one weight per word per queue

5 min live

Naive Bayes estimates its numbers by counting. Logistic regression learns its numbers by trying, being wrong, and adjusting: one weight for every word in every queue, pushed up when the word helps pick the right queue and down when it misleads. The result is a table you can read, and reading it is where you find out what the model actually learned.

LiveSix scores, one softmax, one queue2 min▶

Chapter 4 of Jurafsky and Martin's 2026 draft is titled "Logistic Regression and Text Classification", and the multi-class version is exactly Ledgerly's problem. For each queue the model keeps a weight for every feature (here, TF-IDF weighted words and two-word bigrams that appear in at least two training complaints) plus a bias. A complaint's score for a queue is the bias plus each feature's value times its weight. Softmax turns the six scores into six probabilities that sum to one; the complaint goes to the biggest.

The weights are learned, not counted. The bench starts them at zero and makes 10 passes over the 2,400 training complaints in a seeded random order (stochastic gradient descent, learning rate 1). Each time a complaint is scored, the weights of its words move a little toward the right queue and away from the wrong ones. The seed fixes the order, so you get the same weights every run.

This rung is R4. It routes 467 of 600 held-out complaints correctly, 0.778, with macro F1 0.779: ten more complaints than naive Bayes on the same holdout.

LiveRead the heaviest words before you read the accuracy2 min▶

Open "What the logistic model learned" on the R4 rung. Most of it is reassuring: "debt" 8.96 and "collection" 6.35 for debt collection, "card" 11.08 for credit card, "car" 7.93 and "vehicle" 6.34 for vehicle loan, "bank" 6.81 and "deposit" 5.02 for checking or savings. A model whose top words are the product words is routing for the right reasons.

Two rows deserve a second look. In debt collection, the sixth heaviest word is "report" (2.66), the same word that leads credit reporting (4.40). Collectors report debts to the credit bureaus, so complaints about one often talk about the other; that shared vocabulary is exactly where Part 3's biggest misroute comes from. And in credit card, "for" (2.74) and "case" (2.23) carry weight: not product words, just habits of how card complaints happen to be written in this sample.

Self-studyCompany names are strong signals, and fragile ones3 min▶

Three of the six heaviest student loan words are company names: "mohela" 6.59, "nelnet" 4.47 and "aidvantage" 3.71, all federal student loan servicers. In credit reporting, "experian" (3.53), a credit bureau, sits among the top three. The model is not wrong to use them: in 2023, a complaint naming a federal loan servicer was very likely a student loan complaint.

It is a dependency you did not choose. If loans move to a new servicer, or a servicer starts offering a different product, the name stops meaning what it meant in the training rows and the routing quietly degrades. A product word like "loan" travels; a company name is a fact about the market in one year. Write the company-name weights on the playbook page so the next retrain checks whether they still hold.

Self-studyWhy weights beat counts here, and only by ten complaints2 min▶

Naive Bayes estimates each word's evidence on its own, so two words that always appear together are counted twice. Logistic regression learns all weights at once, so when "credit" and "report" travel together it can split the credit between them. It can also use bigrams like "a_debt" and "credit_card" that session 2 added. That is why it edges ahead: 467 against 457 correct.

Ten complaints out of 600 is a modest gap, and the two models disagree on different complaints. Session 4 puts both on the full bench with validation tuning and two traps, and asks whether the gap survives.

Real world The router that learned the brand, not the problem. An insurer's email classifier routes claims beautifully for a year. Then a partner company is acquired and renamed, and claims mentioning the new name start landing in a general inbox. Nobody had looked at the weights: the old partner's name had been one of the strongest signals for the claims queue. Reading the top words would have flagged it on day one; a quarterly check on name-heavy weights would have caught the rename.

Part 3 · covers per-queue precision, recall, F1 and the confusion table

Errors are a routing cost

5 min live

An accuracy of 0.778 says 133 of 600 complaints went to the wrong queue. It does not say which ones, or which desk is drowning in the wrong work. For routing, every error has a direction: from the queue it belonged to, into the queue it landed in. The confusion table records every direction, and precision and recall summarise each queue.

LivePrecision and recall, in routing words2 min▶

Precision of a queue: of what was routed here, how much belonged here. R4 sends 101 complaints to checking or savings and 73 of them are checking or savings complaints: precision 0.723. The other 28 are someone else's work on that desk.

Recall of a queue: of what belonged here, how much arrived. Of 90 checking or savings complaints, 73 arrived: recall 0.811. The other 17 are sitting on another desk.

F1 is the harmonic mean of the two, one number per queue, and macro F1 averages it over the six queues so that a small queue counts as much as a big one. The full definitions, with the arithmetic, are taught in Intro to ML, b7: classification metrics; here we apply them to queues.

LiveRead the confusion table row by row2 min▶

Each row is a true queue; each column is where its complaints were sent; the diagonal is the right answers. Read the off-diagonal cells of 10 or more first (the bench shades them). For R4 there are three: debt collection to credit reporting (23), card to checking or savings (14), and credit reporting to debt collection (12).

Compare R2 and R4 cell by cell and the story is stable. Debt collection to credit reporting: 21 under naive Bayes, 23 under logistic. Card to checking: 16, then 14. Logistic regression did not fix Ledgerly's two biggest leaks; it shaved errors elsewhere. When two different models make the same mistake, the cause is usually in the text (or the labels), not the model.

Self-studyWhich error costs more is a business decision3 min▶

A debt collection complaint that lands in credit reporting is read by a team that handles bureau disputes, not collection practices. It waits, gets rerouted, and the reply goes out late. A credit reporting complaint that lands in debt collection costs the collections desk some reading time. The counts are 23 and 12, but the costs per complaint are not equal, and no metric knows which one hurts Ledgerly more. The complaints manager does.

That decision changes which number you optimise. If late collection replies are the expensive error, you want debt collection recall up, even at the price of credit reporting precision: a lower threshold for "debt collection", or a rule that sends any complaint with both "debt" and bureau words to a human. Write the decision down with an owner; a model can only follow a cost it has been told.

Self-studyThe rebalanced sample changes every precision2 min▶

The bench's sample is rebalanced: credit reporting is 30% of it, but 76.7% of the real 2023 complaints. Recall is a property of each queue's own rows and travels reasonably well to the real mix. Precision does not: on the real mix, there would be far more credit reporting complaints available to leak into every other queue, so the precision of the small queues would fall and credit reporting's would rise. Before quoting any precision to a manager, rescore on a sample with the real mix, or at least say which mix the number comes from.

Real world The 95% that hid the complaints nobody answered. A bank's complaint router reports 95% accuracy every month. A new operations lead asks for the confusion table and finds that most of the errors flow one way: hardship requests were landing in the general servicing queue, where the response clock is longer. The overall number was true and useless. One row of the table, with an owner, fixed in a week what a year of accuracy reports had hidden.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What does add-one smoothing prevent in naive Bayes?

The score multiplies one likelihood per word. A single zero wipes out the queue whatever the other words say. Adding 1 to every count (alpha 1.0, scikit-learn's default) keeps every word's likelihood above zero.

2 · Under R4, credit reporting has precision 0.789 and recall 0.894. What does that pair tell Ledgerly?

Recall 0.894: of the 180 credit reporting complaints, 161 arrived. Precision 0.789: of the 204 routed there, 43 came from other queues, 23 of them from debt collection. On the real mix, where credit reporting is 76.7% and not 30%, these numbers would shift.

3 · "mohela" (6.59) is the third heaviest word for the student loan queue. Why is that a risk?

The name is a genuine signal in the training year, which is why the model uses it. It is a fact about the market, not about the complaint, so it can expire. Record it as a dependency and check it at every retrain.
